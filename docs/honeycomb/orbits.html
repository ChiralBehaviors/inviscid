Three orbits, and the wall at thirty degrees

Every vertex of a cuboctahedron traces a planar ellipse as the jitterbug opens — verified to 1.4e-16. One triangular face takes one vertex from each of three mutually perpendicular planes, so a face rides three orbits at once. The crank angle is not merely a parameter on those ellipses: it is exactly their eccentric anomaly.

Because every vertex coordinate is a pure sinusoid in a, every plate-to-plate gap along the symmetric path is a sinusoid too. The family that blocks the array comes out as gap(a) = 4·cos(a + 60°), matching the measured gap to 6.7e-16, so first contact falls at exactly 30° — with all sixteen separated plate pairs closing at the same instant rather than in sequence. Past 30° the witness distance stays exactly zero through 45°: the plates touch and remain in contact. It is a boundary of configuration space, not a crossing.

The icosahedron sits inside

a_ico = 22.2388° is interior to the free range, with 7.76° of headroom before the wall. Icosahedral configurations are not at a limit or a singularity — which is why a local group can arrive at that phase and leave again.

The octahedron is unreachable

The VE→octahedron transformation completes at 60°, well past the wall. A packed array of impenetrable plates cannot get there. It stops at half.

The wall belongs to the array

A lone jitterbug does not self-collide at 30° — its own faces are still 1.03 apart, and only close near 59–60°. The limit appears only once neighbours are present.

Scope. Every figure here is a symmetric-path quantity — all units sharing one phase. The simulated array does not stay on that path: it dephases by about 6.7°, the centre unit lagging its neighbours, so the real boundary is softer and arrives earlier than a clean 30°. And none of this speaks to propagation. Rigid impenetrable bodies in unilateral contact form a granular medium, and those carry waves only through collisions, which needs inertia that this quasi-static model does not have.
